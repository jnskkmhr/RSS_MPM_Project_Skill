What the sand under a loaded foot does on a slope, and how each policy steps on it

First pass, 2026-11-23, unchecked. MJWarp-Rough, RFT-Rough and the earlier MPM policy are final; MPM-Rough is aga5ovkp at iteration 16,500 and is rerun on its final checkpoint when the training ends. Beach sand (density 1800 kg/m³, friction 0.80, no cohesion), 0.25 m deep, 10 trials per policy and slope, 1.0 m/s command. The grid, the stance definition, the close-up rule and the failure rule were written down before the runs (task record, "definitions fixed before running").

In one paragraph. H1 holds: the sand under a loaded foot moves downhill by an amount that grows with the slope and is the same for every policy. H2 holds for the outcome but not for the mechanism it suggests: the MPM-trained policy reaches the goal on every uphill slope where the baselines start to fail, yet its foot moves downhill as much per stance as theirs. No policy slid back; the baselines fail by drifting out of the lane or falling forward. What separates the policies is how the foot is placed: the sand-trained policies plant a nearly level sole into the slope, while the rigid-trained policy bears on its toes with the sole 7° to 23° further toes-down and its foot keeps moving over the sand throughout the stance.

Repeatability. This sweep repeats the P1 sweep on the same seed. At the two steepest slopes the goal counts moved by up to three trials between the two runs (MJWarp at +30°: 5 then 3; RFT at +30°: 2 then 5; MPM-Rough at −30°: 10 then 9), so differences of two or three trials at ±30° are within the scatter of one run.

H1: the sand under a loaded foot moves downhill, more on steeper slopes and softer or more slippery sand

Supported. After the foot has dug in, the sand that lay within 3 cm under the sole moves downhill during the rest of the stance by about 0 cm on flat ground (MJWarp-Rough's foot drags it 1.9 cm forward), 1.2–1.3 cm at +10°, 2.4–3.4 cm at +20° and 5.7–7.1 cm at +30° (range over the four policies; panel b). The rank correlation between slope angle and this motion over trials is 0.97 uphill and 0.96 downhill. On a slope the policies differ by about 1 cm at most, so this is a property of the sand under a loaded foot and not of how a policy steps.

The foot is what moves the sand. Around each footprint 6–10 L of sand move more than 1 cm per stance on flat ground, 15–16 L at +20° and 19–20 L at +30° (panel g). In a patch of the same size 0.6 m to the side less than 0.05 L moves from −15° to +25° and at most 0.11 L at +30° (panel h); it moves at −20° (up to 0.2 L), −25° (0.1–0.6 L) and −30° (1.3–1.6 L).

MJWarp-Rough reached 9, 8, 7, 7 and 3 of 10 at +10°, +15°, +20°, +25° and +30°; RFT-Rough 10 of 10 up to +25° and 5 of 10 at +30°. Velocity error of the successful trials at +30°: 0.23 m/s (MPM-Rough), 0.30 (RFT-Rough), 0.38 (MJWarp-Rough). Inside the training range of slopes (up to 21.8°) the three track equally well (0.05–0.18 m/s).

The MPM-trained policy does not slip less. Its bearing point moves downhill by 2.6 cm per stance at +20° and 9.4 cm at +30°, against 4.3 and 8.3 cm for RFT-Rough and −0.4 and 5.4 cm for MJWarp-Rough (panel a). As a share of the step length that is 0.11 and 0.53 for MPM-Rough, 0.21 and 0.49 for RFT-Rough, 0.00 and 0.30 for MJWarp-Rough (panel i). The sand under the sole carries each of them the same distance (panel b). So the policy copes with the moving sand by staying upright and on course while it slips, not by slipping less.

Nobody slides back. Of the 22 failed uphill trials in this sweep, 11 left the lane sideways (all MJWarp-Rough, at +15° to +30°), 7 fell forward (5 RFT-Rough and 1 earlier MPM at +30°, 1 MJWarp-Rough at +10°), 4 collapsed with the pelvis too low (MJWarp-Rough at +30°), and none slid back, stalled or tipped backward. MJWarp-Rough's yaw-rate error grows from 0.37 rad/s on flat ground to 0.6–0.7 rad/s at +10° to +30° (P1 slide), which matches leaving the lane.

Where MPM-Rough is worse. Downhill it tracks the command worst of the three: 0.69 m/s error at −30° against 0.29 (MJWarp-Rough) and 0.33 (RFT-Rough), 0.34 against 0.16 and 0.18 at −25°. Against the earlier MPM policy it is better downhill (1.06 m/s at −30° before) and worse uphill beyond +20° (0.16 and 0.23 m/s at +25° and +30° against 0.08 and 0.14). On rigid ground it is the weakest at the two extremes (table under the sanity checks).

How each policy steps, and whether that explains the smaller slip

What the measurements say about the smaller foot slip of RFT-Rough and MPM-Rough. The slip number of the P1 slide is the speed of the centre of the loaded sole (panel g): 0.43–0.81 m/s for MJWarp-Rough on every slope, 0.06–0.57 m/s for the others. The bearing point of the sole tells the same story (panel h): MJWarp-Rough's covers 0.45–0.86 m/s while the foot is loaded on every slope, flat ground included; for the sand-trained policies it covers 0.08–0.12 m/s on flat ground and rises to 0.58–0.64 m/s at +30°. So the rigid-trained policy's foot never comes to rest on the sand, while the others plant the foot and lose that hold only on steep slopes.

What MJWarp-Rough's foot does. On flat ground its bearing point travels 15 cm forward per stance, 12.7 cm of it in the first 0.06 s after the foot first carries 20 N: the foot is still ploughing forward through the sand when it takes load. Downhill it slides: 17–40 cm downhill per stance at −10° to −30°, of which 7–18 cm are the foot sliding over the sand under it, with stances up to twice as long as the other policies' (0.43–0.63 s). It still reaches every downhill goal, with the smallest or equal-smallest velocity error.

Sole angle goes with it; timing does not. MJWarp-Rough holds its sole 7° to 23° further toes-down than the other policies at every slope, in mid-stance and mostly at touchdown (−20° on flat ground, −48° to the slope at +20°; panels d, e). RFT-Rough and both MPM policies set the sole down parallel to flat ground and, on an uphill slope, nearly level (on the dotted line), so the foot cuts a step into the slope instead of lying on it. Stance duration, step length and step time separate the policies much less uphill: MJWarp-Rough steps a little faster (0.19–0.21 s against 0.21–0.28 s) and the three sand-trained policies differ among themselves by as much.

Loading. On flat ground load comes onto MJWarp-Rough's foot more slowly (half body weight after 0.044 s, against 0.011–0.024 s). From +20° upward every policy reaches half body weight within about one 5 ms physics step, so the loading time cannot separate them there. The largest load in the first 0.1 s is 1.0–2.5 body weights and does not order the policies consistently. The sole's forward speed just before it first takes load (panel i) does not explain the difference either: MPM-Rough lands at 3.3–4.1 m/s on +5° to +20°, as fast as MJWarp-Rough (3.6–4.0 m/s).

So: of the listed quantities, the sole angle goes with the smaller slip; stance duration, step length, step time and loading do not, except the slower loading of MJWarp-Rough on flat ground. These are associations across four policies at fixed slopes, not a test that changes one gait variable at a time. Rank correlations pooled over slopes are in the data file but mix in the effect of the slope itself and are not used here.

Sanity checks for the downhill result

5. Sand of the slope tests. The task's beach_sand preset: density 1800 kg/m³, friction coefficient 0.80, no cohesion, stiff grains; bed 0.25 m deep.

6. Rigid slopes. The earlier 0 of 10 at −10° on rigid ground was a fault of the test, now fixed: the flat-ground task reset the pelvis at a fixed world height, which on a downhill tile is 1.03 m inside the raised start platform. With the reset placed on the terrain surface, every policy reaches 10 of 10 on rigid ground from −20° to +20°. At the extremes each policy is best on the ground it trained on: at +30° MJWarp-Rough reaches 8 of 10 on rigid ground and 3 of 10 on sand, MPM-Rough 0 of 10 on rigid ground (all falls) and 10 of 10 on sand; at −30° MPM-Rough reaches 2 of 10 on rigid ground and 9 of 10 on sand.

Is downhill sand like rigid ground? For the outcome, mostly yes up to −25°: every policy reaches the goal on both grounds and the velocity errors agree within 0.06 m/s (for example MPM-Rough 0.29 on rigid ground and 0.34 on sand at −25°; 0.73 and 0.69 at −30°). The poor downhill tracking of the MPM policies is therefore not caused by the sand; it appears on rigid ground too.

7. Sliding angle. The unloaded bed has no sharp sliding angle in this simulation: its top layer creeps at every tilt, about ten times faster for every 15° (0.03 cm/s at 5°, 0.24 at 20°, 0.80 at 30°, 3.6 at 40° for friction 0.80). By the rule fixed beforehand (top 5 cm faster than 1 cm/s) beach sand slides at 35.0°; a flow faster than 10 cm/s starts at 42.5°; arctan(0.80) is 38.7°. In separate 10 s holds after a sudden tilt the top layer moved 3.6 cm at 20° and 15 cm at 30° and was still creeping at 0.07 and 0.19 cm/s at the end. The slopes of the sweep are thus 0.29 (10°), 0.57 (20°) and 0.86 (30°) of the sliding angle: 30° is not far from it. In the walking tests this creep does not show because sand away from the robot is asleep until something loads it.

8. Sand under the stance foot, downhill against uphill. More sand moves downhill than uphill at the same angle: 4.3–4.9 cm per stance at −20° against 2.4–3.4 cm at +20°, and 7.1–9.4 cm at −30° against 5.7–7.1 cm at +30°; displaced volume 20–22 L against 15–16 L at ±20°.

Verdict on the hypothesis that this sand acts close to rigid ground downhill. Half supported. The outcomes downhill are close to those on rigid ground, and at 10° and 20° the sand is far from sliding. But the sand does not behave rigidly: it moves more under a downhill stance foot than under an uphill one. Downhill that motion is along the walking direction, so it costs no progress; that, and not rigidity, is the simpler reading of why every policy descends.

How to read these results
  • The baselines' 0 of 16 on MPM sand is a walking failure, not a towing failure. Both baseline tow policies also fall there with the rope cut. The results support that towing on MPM sand needs about twice the pull of rigid ground. At 1.0 m/s the ground holds the cube back with 105 N on MPM sand, 50 N on rigid ground and 60 to 66 N on RFT sand; at 0.5 m/s with 93, 49 and 47 to 58 N. RFT sand has the MPM sand's density and friction in every row of this slide. The results do not show that the baselines lack the pulling force.
  • The cube is not 7 cm deep in the MPM sand. The cube heights on this slide are measured from the level the ground is generated at, and loose MPM sand settles about 5 cm below that level on its own. The cube already reads 6.0 cm at rest; towing lowers it by a further 1.3 cm, against 0.5 cm on the RFT sand.
  • The rows with the turning command are not towing along a curve. The robot's circle is shorter than the tether, so the cube pivots near the centre.
  • Earlier deformation of the sand has a small effect in the towing runs. Crossing its own track lowers the ground force on the towed cube by about 6 % and the cube rides about 2 cm deeper, but the laps overlap only partly and the sand can leave sideways. When G1 tows the free cube along a channel as narrow as the cube, where the sand has to pile up in front, the pull rises by about a tenth and the cube climbs over the sand (channel towing test below).
  • The MPM-trained policy is not best everywhere. On RFT sand at 0.5 m/s it reaches the goal in 8 of 16 trials; the other 8 run into the time limit, with a velocity error of 0.34 m/s.
  • Videos exist only for the MPM-trained tow policy, in the channel towing test at the end of this slide; the other two checkpoints have none yet.

How the sand piles up: the material points around the cube

What the level cube and the particle views show
  • Key takeaway: the sand piles up in the G1 task as in the box test once the cube cannot tilt, and then G1 cannot tow it. Held level, the cube digs in (8 cm below the settled surface in the channel and 10 cm on the open bed after 8 to 11 s, still sinking), the pull is 178 N in the first seconds instead of about 105 N, and the cube moves 1.4 m in the channel and 1.5 m on the open bed in the whole trial, against 9.4 m when it is free.

  • The wall matters less than how the cube is held. The level cube stops G1 on the open bed as well as in the channel. In the box-only test, where the pull is as strong as needed, the same cube at the same 7 cm depth pushes a wedge 20 cm high and half a metre long in the narrow bed.

Why the box pulled alone piles up more sand than the box towed by G1

Three differences in how the box is moved
  • The towed box tilts and rides up. It leans back by about 6° and its bottom stays at the sand surface, so it slides over the sand in front of it. The box pulled alone is held level, cannot turn, and sinks 7 to 16 cm while it moves, so its whole front face pushes sand.
  • The tilt decides, not the lift of the rope. The rope rises at 25° and carries 50 N, about a third of the box's weight. Held level, the box digs in although the rope then carries more, 62 to 85 N.
  • G1 can only pull so hard. The box pulled alone gets whatever force keeps it at 0.5 m/s, over 1 kN at the end of the narrow bed, for the full 4.8 m. G1 pulls with 105 to 125 N in steady towing and about 200 N at most; when the box needs more, the box stops, and a box that stops piles up no more sand.
  • Not separated here. The box pulled alone moves at 0.5 m/s on a fixed bed that has rested for 10 s. The towed box moves at up to 1.0 m/s over sand the robot has just walked on. Material and depth of the sand are the same.

What limits these results

  • MJWarp-Rough, RFT-Rough and MPM-Rough come from the earlier code and differ in training recipe; the retrained MPM policy comes from the unified code and is not a final checkpoint.   • Evaluation sand and soft-material values are presets, not fitted to a real terrain.
